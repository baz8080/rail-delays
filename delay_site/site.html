A notice going up is the only signal, and a notice coming down is the only other one. The feed has no "resolved" field: a notice leaving it just means the author took it down, and nothing here says when the trains were running normally again.

What this measures

The same feed of service messages the sibling lift site reads, polled every 30 minutes, minus the lift and escalator outages, which are published there. Nothing before exists here, and days before it are shown as "no data".

One disruption is one event, not one notice. Irish Rail re-words a live notice as things develop - nine wordings over eight hours on 17 August were one closure between Thurles and Limerick Junction - so those are folded into one entry that says how many times it was re-worded.

Trains listed as having reduced capacity are left out. "Customer Notice: This train has reduced capacity" is a template about the seating rather than about a train running late, and on the corpus to September 2026 it was a third of the feed. The count is printed above each month's list, so a third of the subject is never quietly missing.

How to read a disruption

Each entry names the service or the stretch of line the notice is about, when it was first listed, and how often it was re-worded while it stood. Under that are Irish Rail's own words: the headline first, then the sentence beneath it. The tags are what those words named, and a notice can name more than one thing.

Where a notice claims a figure, the entry carries the worst figure that notice ever claimed, prefixed "at least". The feed writes ranges - "+15/20 minutes delayed" - and a range is only a promise at its lower end.

What this cannot tell you

How late the trains were, overall. Some notices carry a figure and most do not, and the ones that do are not the same kind of number: "this service departed +25 minutes behind schedule" is one train observed, while "+90 minute delays can be expected" is a forecast over many. Adding them up would also count one event several times, because the figure is re-stated every time the notice is re-worded. So each disruption carries the worst figure it ever claimed, and nothing anywhere is added up.

What share of trains ran late. Irish Rail names a service only when something is wrong with it. There is no published roll of the services that ran, so there is nothing to divide by: this site can say how many disruptions were listed and never what share of trains were late. Any such figure would be invented.

When a disruption ended. A notice leaving the feed means somebody took it down, which is not the same fact as the trains running normally again, so nothing here measures how long anything lasted.

How a cause is read

Each notice usually names a cause in its own words - "due to a signalling issue", "following an incident on the line". Those words are read into a set of categories drawn from what the feed actually writes, and a notice can name more than one.

Nothing is decoded. "An incident on the line" is published as that and no more, because the notice says no more. "An operational issue" is filed as no cause given, because that is what it is. And a notice blaming another delay is marked as doing so rather than counted beside a fault.
